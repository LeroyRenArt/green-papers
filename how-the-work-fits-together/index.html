How the work fits together

How an observation can change a question, a relationship and the conditions for caring for a place.

When the question changes

Imagine a group of growers and stewards looking after a nursery through a dry spell. Some plants are wilting, and watering is taking more time than the group agreed to set aside. One person points to the afternoon sun. Another points to the days when water is hardest to collect. A third asks which plants make sense to grow here in the first place. They could start by watching a few beds and seeing what changes. They might also find that their first question was too narrow.

This is an imagined example of how an inquiry can branch out when more people have a chance to shape it.

A grower farther up the catchment might know about a change in how water is allocated. An ecological adviser might draw attention to habitats the group’s usual rounds miss. A steward might show that saving water has shifted work onto someone else. Each perspective gives the group something different to investigate. Watering still matters, but the inquiry might now include which species can find room here, who can keep up the care, and who has the authority to change access to water.

How do we tell the explanations apart?

This is where citizen science can help. The participants frame their own question. They decide which differences are worth following, who could help them make sense of those differences, and what would count against their current explanation. A working wager makes an expectation explicit before the group knows what will happen. When they return, they may find the expectation was too narrow, or that they need a different way to tell the explanations apart.

If the group wishes, it can give AI a specific task. It might ask an AI service to compare two public texts on shade and water use, point to the passages it draws on, and suggest an observation that could weaken each explanation. The group can read those passages and judge whether the suggestions fit the place. A specialist or neighbour can challenge both the AI’s answer and the group’s assumptions. That is critical friendship when another perspective has a real chance to change the direction of the inquiry. Using private or shared material in an AI service requires separate permission. The group can also pursue the inquiry through conversations, site visits and a notebook.

What do we need to remember together?

The Field Diary gives each person somewhere to record their own experience and choose what to bring to the group. In the PG Ledger, the group can connect selected records over time. What did we see? What did we base our decision on? Who did the work, and what followed? An observation sheet, a decision note or a financial record can each help the group remember something specific. When participants return, they can see whether to revise the explanation, change the task or reopen the agreement. That is how a return visit becomes part of a learning spiral.

The plants might be doing better while one steward has taken on more unpaid work. Penguin Economics offers an image of how exposure and protection can shift. In practice, someone else might take on a task, with the time and authority to do it. Stewardship economy takes the question further. What work does the place need, who can do it, and what needs funding to make that possible? A return visit can show whether the new division of work is helping and whether the living things at the site now have the conditions the group hoped to support.

Who can respond, and what might change?

Over time, the stewards can build a basis for deciding whom to approach. If they can show that the grant covers setting up the nursery, while watering and care need time week after week, they can ask the funder to revisit the agreement. If access to water depends on several users, an inquiry they undertake together may change who needs to be involved in finding a solution. Another group may get in touch to share an experience that opens up a new question. A shared responsibility or paid role might emerge that no one had imagined at the start. The stewards need to be able to distinguish a contact from a reply, a change in a decision, and a difference they can see when they return.

This is one way to understand field intelligence. People and institutions can reshape one another’s inquiries through their work with a living place. Learning makes a connection visible. Decision-making authority determines who can change the conditions. Funding can give someone the time and resources to make the change happen. What happens next can change how the group and the other parties understand the situation.

What can travel further?

A Local Return might be an answer the group can use in its next piece of work. Approaching a specialist, funder or public authority calls for a separate decision about what is relevant to share with that recipient. If the field later wants to share something more widely, it can choose a Field Return with a clear scope. It may be an account of what the group learned or a question it wants others to help with. Where the contribution came from, what remains uncertain and the terms of use need to travel with it. Those involved must be able to approve the account and correct it.

The Field Returns shelf makes room for what a field chooses to share and for a response to find its way back. The Flow and Return Protocol describes these different ways of sharing and returning in more detail.
