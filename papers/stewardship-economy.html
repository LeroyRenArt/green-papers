1. The life we make room for

A habitat is changing, and a species may lose what it needs to survive there. A community has to act before drought costs it another harvest. Others begin because they want to grow food, learn or make something together. Often people are already carrying responsibility before they have time to frame a question. We can see how some of these things come together on a farm.

Imagine a farm in late summer. A grower walks along two rows of pomegranate trees. At the field margin, most of the young trees still have leaves. Further in, some are drying out. Soon she will have to decide whether to commit another season’s water and labour to the whole orchard. The harvest is part of her family’s income. On the same walk she sees that there are still flowers along the wild edge, when most of the field is dry. She goes home with two questions. What is keeping the trees alive? What else could she make room for along the edge?

Our working definition of stewardship economy is a way of organising work, resources and decision-making that gives real weight to the needs of people, species and habitats in economic choices. It asks what would make care possible over time and how experience could change the arrangements that shape those conditions. It concerns access to land and water, ownership and use rights, time, money and risk. Care includes protecting what remains and making room for life to recover or emerge. Species and habitats matter in their own right. People also need time, room to act and a livelihood that allows them to keep caring for a place.

Who benefits from what a place provides, and who is left to bear the cost? A harvest brings in money, while growers are left to deal with pressure on water supplies and keep the soil alive. A grant can pay for installation, leaving maintenance to people whose time was never budgeted for. Research can carry local knowledge out into the world while the people who made it possible are still waiting for a useful answer. An arrangement becomes extractive when it can repeatedly draw out value while the costs, risks or loss of control fall elsewhere. That question applies to our own work with knowledge as well. Those who have benefited can help pay for the care and repair now needed. And the decisions that keep generating the cost can change. Paying people to care for a wetland matters. If the wetland needs less water to be withdrawn before it can recover, that decision matters too.

For a household trying to secure its next harvest or spending much of the day fetching water, time to care for a place and investigate what is happening there has to come from somewhere. Soil, water and habitats may need several seasons under less pressure before a change becomes visible. An agreement has to make sense for the coming season and for those who will live with it after us. What work becomes possible now? What will have time to recover? Who can carry responsibility forward?

A different arrangement can also make things possible that people discover only as they work together. A farm may become a place to learn. Several households may find a shared way to keep water and plants available. An organisation may discover a task that falls between its programmes. The inquiry follows who gains room to act, who does the work and what changes for species and habitats.

How can experience from living places change the way we share time, money, access, risk and responsibility? People need to be able to act on what they learn while retaining control over what they share. Their experience needs a route to those who can change the conditions, and the answer needs a route back. When they return to the place or the work, they can ask what the change meant for the place, for those doing the work and for others affected.

The texts on Papers have themselves taken shape through a practice we are investigating. AI helped draft and revise the prose. A human knowledge steward brought experiences and questions into the work, chose its direction, challenged the syntheses and took responsibility for the public form. Sophia Lumen Protocol

Start with what you notice

Perhaps you have seen young trees dry out while those along the edge do better. A place you know has changed. Or you want to know whether something you already do is helping. Start with what you noticed, where and when. Write a few words, speak into your phone or take a photo for your own use. Choose one thing to look for next time. Use the Field Diary and open tools or keep your own note. If you have access to AI, it can help you find a relevant reading or sharpen the question. You decide what to share. 2. The seasons of water and the timing of money

Next time, the grower takes a neighbour out with her. The trees at the edge are in shade for part of the afternoon, with leaves and cuttings on the ground. Between the inner rows, the ground is bare. Her neighbour asks whether both rows were watered during the hottest days. They choose a few trees to follow in each place, feel the soil after watering and note when it dries. They also want to see what already lives along the margin. A hedge could add to the existing vegetation. Before choosing plants or clearing anything, they need to see what the edge already offers in the way of flowers, shelter and breeding places.

The soil and water bear traces of years of use. The grower’s observations can be read alongside neighbours’ experience, historical traces and studies of species, soils and the catchment. A dry season, changes in water withdrawal elsewhere and a new practice on the farm can all leave a mark on the same orchard. If water use per tree falls, total withdrawal still matters. And a species may depend on a habitat being available for a short period when it breeds or feeds.

In Doukkala, Morocco, a proposed farm inquiry starts from two areas on the same holding. In an existing pomegranate orchard the question is what to build on, and how new plants might affect the trees’ access to light and water. On open ground there is more room to shape an agroforestry system from the start, and more to establish and care for through the first dry season. The holding also has sheep, goats, beehives and water storage. Where do animals need shade? What feeds honeybees and wild pollinators through the season? What can be added within the water available? A possible first cycle is in local preparation. Doukkala field record

An established farm school could contribute to developing another place for learning. Imagine a visit that starts with a question about hedge plants. Some of the visitors know how to propagate plants others would have to obtain from far away. An experienced grower can show what it takes to get young plants through a dry season. That could lead to an agreement to pay for propagation, teaching or continued support. It might also change which species the farms can work with and how much they can take on. Before it becomes shared work, people need to know who wants to carry it, where the time and water will come from and what each participating place will get back.

An intervention may need several seasons, while wages, rent and repayments fall due along the way. Who can afford a period of lower income? Who pays for observation and care long before any improvement becomes visible? If more water becomes available or demand for the farm’s produce grows, who benefits and what pressure might follow elsewhere?

What a payment can sustain

A sale brings in money for the fruit. A payment for work can cover time, specialist help or habitat care. Shared funds can support cooperation and observation across holdings. A grant can make room for an inquiry whose result is still open. These can coexist. Each arrangement needs to make clear what work the money enables, what obligations come with it, who bears the risk and who decides. A habitat’s value reaches beyond what anyone will pay to care for it. A better income tells us little about the species. More habitat says little by itself about whether a grower can keep doing the work. A good agreement on one farm can coexist with rising pressure downstream. The condition of the place, the steward’s ability to continue and the quality of shared governance need separate attention. Green Paper 23

Imagine a local foundation offers to pay for establishing a living hedge in the first year. The grower begins where it can connect with wild vegetation. Some young plants dry out in the first summer. When she and a neighbour return after another season, there is more cover and they see insects feeding at the flowers. They want to see whether the edge remains useful through the dry period. Care takes longer than expected, and young plants need water. What will the hedge use during establishment, and how might it change the field’s water needs later? Across the catchment, withdrawal has risen. The growers do not yet know how much is due to drought, a change on the farm or other water users.

The grower compares notes with two neighbours. One covered the soil too, but his trees showed signs of stress earlier. On the other farm the trees held on longer. She remembers getting water just before the hottest days. The group uses AI to assemble the notes they chose to share into a timeline. A neighbour spots that the model put two waterings in the wrong week. He finds the original note and they correct it. An adviser points out that soil type, tree age and variety could also matter. They decide to follow more comparable trees next season. They also want to know why water came at different times. The trees can only tell them part of the story.

They agree to take the timeline and their question about allocation to the water authority. Personal notes stay on the farms. The growers ask how water was allocated in the hottest weeks and whether records exist for total withdrawal. An answer might help them plan the next season. It could also prompt the authority to investigate effects on other growers or water-dependent habitats. The effects need to be followed separately.

When less water changes the aim

Imagine a later technical review suggests that a low-lying wetland has a reasonable chance of recovery only if total withdrawal falls. The grower has been asking how to keep the orchard going. Now she has to ask how much of its current operation the catchment can sustain if the wetland is to have a chance to recover. Which rows could her family keep in production with less water? What might happen to the rest? The hedge may still offer food and shelter along the margin. The wetland raises another question, one the growers need to take up with the water authority and other water users.

An illustrative five-year estimate. These figures are illustrative. The foundation pays DKK 30,000 for the hedge in year one. If maintenance costs DKK 8,000 a year from establishment, the grower must find DKK 40,000 over five years. The first DKK 8,000 is needed immediately and the remaining DKK 32,000 after the grant expires. If part of the orchard is converted and the farm is left with DKK 10,000 less after variable costs in each of the first two years, that adds DKK 20,000. The gap is DKK 60,000 before any new income or savings.

The foundation can decide whether it will fund care over several seasons and help the family through a period of lower income. The water authority has a different task. It has to consider what total withdrawal means for the wetland and what can change in water allocation. On returning, growers and specialists can look for signs that pressure on the wetland has eased, check whether the species they follow use the hedge across the season, and see whether work and income still add up for the people who need to continue.

What can stewardship economy contribute where conservation, farm advice and water management already exist? Each may have the knowledge, responsibility and tools to carry part of the work. The inquiry follows what can fall between their boundaries and time horizons. An action can work on one holding and still affect habitats or water beyond it. A grant can pay for establishment while others carry the work and risk after it ends. Who benefits, who bears the consequences, and who can change the agreement or rule? An existing arrangement that already connects these decisions and consequences is also an answer. The question about boundaries and time horizons comes from a study of Danish land-use change. Here it is brought to an imagined example inspired by Doukkala. Finding out how it plays out there would take work on the ground. At holde jorden levende · Danish

Try the question where you are. Have you noticed something doing better in one place than another? Record what you saw. Choose a difference to look for next time, such as shade, ground cover or when water was applied. Ask someone else what they noticed. If you use AI, ask it to separate observations from possible explanations and show its sources. You can come back with a better question while the explanation is still open.

3. How a place finds a direction

How different fields of practice find a direction

Conservation, ecological restoration and rewilding can look at the same landscape and ask different questions. Species conservation might start with a declining population and ask what needs protection now and what might allow the population to breed and spread again. The IUCN Red List concerns extinction risk. Its Green Status examines species recovery and the role of conservation. A global assessment can orient a local inquiry. Local presence and habitat quality still require their own investigation. IUCN Red List · Green Status of Species

Ecological restoration might begin by asking which species, functions or connections an ecosystem has lost. Ecological references help in judging what might recover and under what conditions. Rewilding gives particular weight to processes such as succession, dispersal and natural disturbance shaping what happens next. Nature-based solutions may begin with a problem such as flooding or heat and ask whether an intervention can address it and benefit biodiversity. These starting points help clarify what we want to protect, what functions might recover, and where we can make room for developments we can only partly foresee. SER restoration standards · IUCN rewilding guidelines · IUCN standard for nature-based solutions

How people find a direction together

People who live and work in a place may have experience of many seasons and feel changes in their health, daily work and livelihood. A specialist may see that a habitat needs less grazing. A family may need fodder this week. The family may know possibilities and constraints that an ecological assessment misses. The assessment may reveal consequences for species or water that are difficult to see from a single holding. Both kinds of knowledge can change the question and bring others’ responsibilities into view.

  1. What is here now? Which species, habitats and processes can we describe with confidence? What remains unknown?
  2. What is already changing? What is recovering or being lost? What might reflect seasonal variation, earlier interventions or changes elsewhere?
  3. What helps us see what could return? A baseline describes the place at the start of the work. Local memory, historical traces and comparable habitats can help us see what was lost and what might find room again.
  4. What is urgent, and what needs time? What can be protected now? What may recover if pressure eases? Who can act, who can bear the cost and when can we return to see what happened?

A beginning might be an endangered species, a farm that needs to keep going, a community under pressure, a city’s dependence on a landscape, a wish to learn together, or an institution discovering the effects of its own practices. Beginnings can change as people work. What can be investigated depends partly on what people already have to do. Land, Water and Living Stewardship · Healthy Roots

Investigating your own place

A grower who wants to know why some trees withstand drought better can begin in her own words. A short reading, a technical paper or a conversation with AI may help her find something worth following. Given access to relevant material, AI can locate passages, compare notes over seasons, translate and bring differences between explanations into view. Even one passage of a long text can help someone shape a better question. That question can lead back to the place, to a neighbour or specialist, or to a trial the group wants to carry out.

This is one possibility Spiralweb explores as AI-supported citizen science. People can form their own questions, try out explanations and revise what they do. In the proposed Learning with Living Fields programme, groups would spend the first month making observations and shaping questions without generative AI. Later they could choose to use it with material they had authorised. Paid time, language support and specialist input are part of the proposal. One question is who can actually do this knowledge work and use it in their own circumstances. Spiralweb glossary

When the first task is to protect what remains

Fire, flooding or drought building over several seasons can force people to act immediately. They may need to keep animals, crops or a nursery alive, or protect a habitat while there is still time. A fuller inquiry can begin later or grow alongside the work already under way.

The Kitgum field record describes nursery preparation, watering and visits to households already growing food under drought pressure. Larger planting awaits rain and local judgement. What needs to be kept alive now? What possibilities can be kept open for next season? Which conditions need to change if the emergency keeps returning? Kitgum field record

Imagine a walk in Kal Central A, a community in northern Uganda. People begin where households fetch water and grow food. One family needs to secure the next crop. Another knows which trees survive dry weeks. The people tending the nursery can say when seedlings need most water and when care competes with other work. The group might try ground cover in a few places and ask how to share watering, care and access to the plants so households with the least spare time also benefit.

They may find that the same households carry most of the water, although many hope to benefit from the plants. There is now a shared question. What can they keep alive together while waiting for rain, and how can they share the work? Before expanding the nursery or investing in water storage they can follow the time and water used now. If a local organisation will handle support, it needs to be able to receive the funds, pay for agreed work and keep accounts. Returning later, households can see what survived, who benefited and whether the new arrangement helped those who had carried the most.

The timing of support matters. The same sum can make more of a difference before a critical period has passed. Available money does not make it sensible to plant before conditions can support it. Kitgum brings a question back to the farm agreement. Can it allow people to act quickly, wait when the place needs them to wait, and pay for keeping options open meanwhile?

4. When experience changes the conditions

When the grower tells the foundation that care takes more time than agreed, a shared account can show which tasks grew and what that means for the agreement. She can keep private the experiences she drew on to understand the strain. Sharing, translating, combining accounts or processing them with AI requires agreement on purpose, content and recipients from those who hold authority over the material. The foundation can receive what it needs to decide on longer-term care.

An observation about water levels might raise a different question. Does it belong to one farm’s practice or point to a catchment pattern? Could growers, specialists and water managers investigate it together? If the decision lies elsewhere, the question needs a route to those who can change the rule or funding. The grounds for a claim, its actual influence and the authority to decide are distinct.

We use pixelation as an image for connecting observations without losing the details that let people return to them. A pixel is an observation or a small inquiry that people can trace back to where and when it took place, what it drew on and what remains uncertain. Several farms may see a shared pattern in water conditions. They may also discover that the same words describe different varieties, ways of farming or room to act. A difference can change the question as much as a resemblance. Places with less time, access or freedom to share can be less visible in the common picture.

Field intelligence is the shared ability to notice, understand and respond. It is distributed across people, sources, methods and what the place itself shows. AI can connect traces and suggest explanations. A critical friend can ask what was actually seen, what else could explain it and how to check. A participant can correct a summary, a specialist can challenge a causal account and a public authority can clarify its remit. Agreement between models gives no independent confirmation. A well-grounded open question can already matter publicly. Atlas · Sophia Lumen Protocol

An agreement can make work possible. What people learn in doing it can give grounds to change the agreement, a rule or an institutional judgement. A reply can also be a reasoned refusal or a decision to keep the question open. People need to receive it where the question began and decide what it means for their next action.

When an organisation needs to change its own work

The hedge made a cost visible when someone had to care for it. A similar shift can occur when a new procedure appears to save a municipality time. Imagine a member of a conservation team responding to a question about wetland care. She finds a previous agreement in a colleague’s notes, checks it with operations staff and asks the local care group for a map it has already sent. The case closes on time. A week later the same thing happens. If the next person in the role faces the same detours, the procedure itself needs attention.

The team could examine a few cases staff choose to bring. How long did it take to find an agreement? Who had to send material again? Who could answer? A manager might make one role responsible for keeping the current agreement available and responding to questions. The team can return later to see whether the work got easier or simply fell to operations staff or the local group, and whether people can still exercise judgement and raise concerns.

A worker can use green, amber or red to describe how the load feels over time. This is a self-assessment whose sharing remains their choice. If entrusted to a conversation about working conditions, it cannot be repurposed for assessing individuals. A common record can hold the condition to be investigated, what changed and who should respond. The worker can raise a concern while deciding what to share about their own experience. Whether the organisation became more responsive can be seen in what changed and how the work feels on returning. Municipal Work as Nature

The municipal example brings a question back to the farm. If a funding arrangement seems efficient because someone takes on documentation or care in their own time, who carried the saving? Returning to the work can reveal where it went, whether into another department or into a household.

When the city helps shape the landscape

The chinampas, cultivated plots between canals in Xochimilco, open a question at another scale. How does a city depend on a living water and food landscape, and how does it shape that landscape in turn? Growing food, maintaining waterways and the conditions for species can be affected by decisions about water, land and public work made in different places. The collaboration is developing. What can be investigated together depends on local priorities and agreements. Xochimilco field record

Imagine a grower has trouble taking tools and vegetables along a canal during part of the season. On the same stretch a neighbour wants to know what clearing the canal would do to bank vegetation and the animals that use it. Their experience gives them different reasons to take a closer look. When is passage difficult? How do the stretches differ? What do growers know about earlier maintenance and water supply? A water specialist can help distinguish what can be addressed there from conditions that depend on the wider canal system.

In its programme proposal, the local organisation IAX describes how four areas of work could connect. They concern grower education, applied research, demonstration of cultivation practices and women’s organisation and agency. A soil test may suggest a change that is straightforward on a demonstration plot. A household may need time, transport or materials it must pay for before any income comes in. If several families describe the same difficulty, IAX may find work falling between teaching and technical advice. Who follows what households try after a course ends? Who gets paid time to bring that experience back to the next group? If the decisive problem recurs along several canals, growers and IAX may have a question to take to the city.

A map of passage, seasons and earlier maintenance could help them ask who is responsible for particular stretches, what has already been budgeted and how habitats are considered. Another inquiry could start with the harvest. If growers lose time and income through transport and irregular arrangements, buyers or a shared route to market may have room to act. A regular supply agreement then raises questions about price, seasonality and who carries the risk if supplies fail. These strands have different parties and money flows. Both affect whether households can make a living and still have time to care for the place.

For Mexico City, the cultivated wetland raises questions about food supply, water management and space for wild species. Flooding, subsidence and pressure on land call for inquiries at city or catchment scale. Who benefits from the landscape remaining alive and in use? Where do the tasks growers and their organisations have to make time for belong in the city’s budgets and agreements? A conversation becomes part of the spiral when it produces something people can take back and revisit, such as a reasoned decision, a maintenance agreement with a responsible party, or a refusal that makes the obstacle visible. What happened to the water and habitats? Could families sustain the work? Which conditions did institutions actually change?

In Morocco, farms and farm schools can ask what soils, habitats and cultivation might recover within water limits and which questions belong to the catchment. In Kal Central A, the inquiry begins with what households can grow and keep alive and which water arrangements they can build and maintain together. In Xochimilco, growers and learning groups can influence teaching and cultivation and raise questions for city water management. These relationships are developing. Effects on nature, livelihoods and public arrangements have to be followed through the work and on returning to it. AnchorPoints

5. When places answer back

Sustaining a relationship over time

Several seasons can pass between an observation, a decision and its effects. Someone has to remember the agreement, keep in touch, receive corrections and return, even if money comes late or a trial pauses. That time is part of the work. Otherwise the relationship depends on the same person keeping it going. Questions from elsewhere can also change what is worth following locally. A specialist may notice that a species uses a field margin at a particular time. A neighbour may show that water shortages on one farm coincide with changes in distribution further up. Together they can see something that changes both what to look for and who to talk to.

Place-based learning spirals

When people work with a place over several seasons, they may discover that something they have been trying to deal with separately is a shared task. It might begin with drying trees or a habitat slowly disappearing. Following what happens can clarify what people can do themselves, who else they need and which decisions have to change elsewhere. New agreements about land, water, time and money can take shape. On returning, people can see whether those agreements made room for more life and gave those doing the work a better chance of continuing.

We use place-based learning spirals as a working name for this way of investigating and acting together. A farm school may discover that a habitat crosses several holdings. One grower can make space, another propagate plants, and the school help follow what survives. If water remains the main constraint, other users and the responsible authority have a part in the matter. The constitutional dimension lies in how the work changes who has a task in common, what they can decide, and who has the time, money and responsibility to see it through. The water, plants and species can show whether a new arrangement is helping.

The 38 working questions grew from dialogue with the fields and AI-assisted reading across Papers. The number records a point in that work. Growers, neighbours, teachers, specialists and the Association can use the questions to see sides of a matter they have yet to discuss. Those carrying a shared inquiry can choose, change or add a question. A local Working Wager puts an expectation into words so people can return to it. What did we think would happen? What would make us think again? A local expectation can also challenge a proposition in the common register. The critique framework follows value and cost through the same situation. Who benefited, who did the work, and did the pressure ease or move? Returning to the work can give grounds to change a local agreement, the Association’s support or the questions and wagers on Papers.

A field contact can keep a relationship going. A diary can hold what was observed. An agreed return can give something useful back locally or open a conversation elsewhere. A decision record can show who took responsibility and when a response is due. People affected by the work need to be able to limit and correct how these tools are used.

An Anchor Point is a locally rooted relationship through which people can shape and revisit a question over time. A field record shows what is under way in a particular place. A farm inquiry in Doukkala and a growing site near Had Soualem with its own water question can learn from one another while retaining their different starting points, pace and local authority. The relationships are real while their shared form is still developing. The Association can carry bounded agreements, coordination and resources. Holding the connection through a rainy season, a pause or an institutional decision requires time and a mandate. AnchorPoints · Shared Capacity

What guides the work and what may change

The value of species and habitats in their own right and the protection of entrusted experience are commitments. Whether the way of working helps us honour them is open to inquiry. A Working Wager is a reasoned, provisional expectation with an account of what would make us think differently. An observation made on returning can give grounds to revise an expectation, a practice or an agreement.

Protected feedback. If people can limit what they share and get an answer about the conditions they raise, repeated strains may be noticed and addressed sooner. The test is whether people can decline without losing room to act, and whether the next person in the role still faces the same strain.

Experience from places and institutional response. If experience reaches someone with a mandate and their answer can be followed back, economic and public arrangements may become more responsive to living conditions. The test is what actually changes, who bears the consequences and what a return visit shows for people, species and habitats.

Learning across places. When local groups compare what they find and keep their differences and uncertainties visible, they may discover a pattern, an exception or a shared constraint they could only see in part from one place. The test is whether participants gain room to inquire and act, and whether those whose experience is represented can correct the common account. Full Working Register

Spiralweb’s own agreements, formats and routes to support must be able to change in response. If a synthesis only feeds a public story, if reporting takes time away from care, or if nobody has the time or mandate to answer, Spiralweb’s own arrangements need another look. It may mean simplifying a form, revising an agreement, moving funds or giving an existing way of working more room.

Spiralweb’s formation is one documented process in which a human knowledge steward worked with AI to build, connect and revise a public body of material. Learning with Living Fields is a programme proposal to investigate what several groups might be able to do together. A workspace held by participants can preserve observations, sources, versions, objections and agreements across conversations and seasons. A private diary, a shared record, research material and a public Field Return serve different purposes and have different access conditions. AI and human responsibility

The work needs to be revisited if fluent prose is given more weight than its grounds, if places become less recognisable to those who live there, or if the work depends on hidden labour or an increasingly burdensome centre. Protection can fail when entrusted experience is used for a new purpose. A response can fail to come when nobody with a mandate takes up the matter. A change can have unforeseen effects even when the protections and the decision process held. Each calls for a different correction.

On the next visit to the farm, growers may have a different question, a revised agreement or new information about the catchment. They can use what they learned locally. If something is ready to travel by agreement, it might give other growers something to investigate, prompt an institution to reconsider a decision, or change an open tool on Papers. People may disagree about values and action. A specific shared question gives them somewhere to begin.

The 2019 Planetary Guardians manifesto already invited people to make more room for wild nature and share what they learned through the work. Early agroforestry work in Morocco gave that invitation a place to begin. In Spiralweb the question has taken on a way of working that brings people back to the place when a trial confounds expectations, a public body needs to respond or a grant ends before the work does. Papers gives readers something to take into their work. At an Anchor Point those holding the relationship can give their learning a form useful to them. Their own account, a walk with a specialist or a short film can open a conversation. The shelf for public Field Returns makes room for learning that participants choose to share after working it through. It can show what had to change in the care of a place, who ended up doing the work or which decision was reopened. The library can learn something that only became knowable through the work.

This is how knowledge work becomes part of stewardship economy. It can change who has time and access to investigate, whose experience counts and who has a chance to respond. The next turn can show what came back and what it meant for living conditions and the people who carry the work.

Reading guide · Eight places to begin

More than one of these beginnings may matter in the same place. Each gives you a question to carry into what you are already doing.

  1. A species or population may be lost. What has been seen, and what do you know about the habitat it needs? Consider who should know the location if it is sensitive. IUCN Red List · Green Status
  2. A degraded place needs a direction. Which function or habitat would you give a chance to recover? Describe the present condition and find a historical or ecological reference that can help. Land, Water and Living Stewardship
  3. You want to make room for natural processes. Which pressures need to ease, what will you follow as succession, dispersal or other processes unfold, and who can sustain care while the outcome remains open? IUCN rewilding guidelines
  4. Farming has to change and someone needs to make a living. Choose one change and follow soil, water or species as well as time, cost and yield. Who has the room to try it? Healthy Roots · Green Paper 23
  5. Something needs attention now. What matters most to keep alive or protect, and who can help locally? Return when there is time to ask what might change before it happens again. Kitgum field record
  6. You want to compare observations from several places. What can be compared? Which differences in place, season, method or observation effort should stay visible? Let a significant difference change the question. Knowing From the Ground
  7. Others want to use knowledge you hold. Decide what can travel, who can give permission and what answer or benefit should come back. An open protocol can be adapted while the experience stays with those who hold it. Knowledge in Right Relationship
  8. You have responsibility for a question that crosses places. What part of water management, financing or the division of work can you actually change? Whose experience could challenge your institution’s reading, and how can they get an answer? Institutional Bridge · Xochimilco field record

If you provide funding. Ask whether the money covers establishment, ongoing care, paid time for learning or the work of keeping a shared agreement alive. Who carries the work when the grant ends? Shared Capacity

What you can do on your own and with others

The open texts and formats can help you turn a broad concern into a question you can follow, follow a change and remember why you chose to act. Use the Field Diary, Healthy Roots or PG Ledger formats as needed. AI can help locate sources and connections. You can return to the place and decide what is worth following. That return can already give something useful locally.

If more people want to work together, an Anchor Point can hold the relationship in which a question is shaped and revisited. Participants can agree what to examine, what specialist help is relevant and what might come back as a Local Return. A Field Return calls for a separate choice about what can be shared. Where a decision lies elsewhere, it may be worth approaching the institution with a mandate to change it.
