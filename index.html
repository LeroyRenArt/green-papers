Learning with the living world

What does it mean for a place to recover? A farm can grow more food while losing species. A canal can be cleaned while pollution continues to flow into it. A place can begin to recover while the people caring for it can no longer afford to carry on. What would it take for that recovery to last? This library grew around questions like these.

By the living field we mean a place together with the relationships and decisions through which actions have consequences. It includes soil, water, species and people, along with the agreements, working practices and decisions that shape their conditions. Water crosses property boundaries, so a farm’s living field can extend across a catchment. It can also include a grant agreement, because the length of the grant helps determine who can keep tending what has been started. A grower, a biologist and a water authority may each know a different part of the same field. Each can help the others see what they might have missed, especially when they return to see what has changed.

We call work regenerative when it helps restore the conditions in which life can thrive. That can involve restoration, maintenance or protection. It can also involve removing pressure so that life can recover on its own. To say what that means in practice, we need to describe the starting conditions, which species, habitats or people we expect to benefit, where we expect to see change and how long it might take. Species and habitats matter in their own right.

From the fields to the library and back

On spiralweb.earth you can meet the people and places that sustain this work. People can follow a question from their own place across several seasons and use the Field Diary, recording formats and AI to compare their observations with specialist knowledge. What they learn should first serve the people doing the work. They decide what can be shared and on what terms.

On Papers, shared experience can meet published work, working hypotheses and experience from elsewhere. Others can take those questions and tools back into their own practice. If the findings point to a problem with access to water, the length of a grant or how work is shared, they can also reach those with the authority to change the terms. On their next visit, participants can see what a response or a new agreement meant for the place and for those doing the work. This is part of the inquiry into stewardship economy.

Two spirals in one library

The first spiral grew out of an artistic inquiry into attention, embodied experience and responsibility. What do we learn to notice? What do we grow accustomed to overlooking? What happens to our judgement under sustained pressure? These questions are still with us. How much someone can reasonably take on matters when we decide whether a work agreement can hold over time.

The second spiral follows those questions into landscapes, organisations and economic decisions. It asks how restoring a place can become a livelihood, how specialist knowledge and lived experience can inform each other, and how those who fund or regulate the work can change their own practice. Some connections become clearer when you read across both spirals.

Stewardship economy · How experience can change the terms of the work

We use stewardship economy as a working term for organising work, resources and decision-making authority so that economic choices give real weight to what species, habitats and people need to thrive. It asks who has access to land and water, who has time to care for a place and who gets paid for that work, and who carries the risk if an agreement falls short. It also asks where the value a place creates ends up and who pays for its recovery.

This changes what we ask in a learning spiral. Before we begin, we say what we expect to happen and what it will take to do the work. What do we hope will change? Who will do the work? What time, support and authority will they need? When we return, we keep three questions open. What happened to the species and habitats? Can the people doing the work continue? Did those affected have a say in the decisions that shaped it? Improvement in one area can accompany deterioration in another. Both need to be visible.

What people learn should first serve those doing the work and living with its consequences. If participants choose to share it, a funder may have reason to extend a grant or a public authority may reopen an agreement. The next visit can then examine what that change made possible. This is how a learning spiral can affect the distribution of money, work and responsibility.

Stewardship economy · Who pays for the work of caring for a place?

We can pay to plant a forest and leave its future care unfunded. That is an economic question as much as an ecological one. Stewardship economy asks how caring for soil, water, species and habitats can become a livelihood. It asks who benefits from the richness of a place, who bears the cost of restoring it, and which losses money can never make good. Can what people learn by doing the work change what we fund next time?

Restoration · What life can return?

A landscape can grow greener while making less room for its wild species. To decide what to do, ask what life the place once supported, what changed, and what can recover under today’s climate and land use. Historical records, local memory and ecological surveys can point to different answers. Some places need active restoration. Elsewhere, removing a source of pressure can give species and habitats room to recover. What is coming back, and how can we tell over time?

Citizen science · Who gets to ask the question?

Someone who grows food, cares for a place or spends time there can notice a change before it appears in an official account. What happens to that observation? Does it become a data point in someone else’s inquiry, or can the person who noticed it help decide what to investigate? Participants can frame questions, invite specialists in and return to see whether their first interpretation holds. They can also say when a professional explanation misses what is happening.

A library you can work with

Citizen science matters here because people who live and work in a place can help decide what to investigate, follow the question over time and bring in people with other kinds of expertise. In a learning spiral, you make your expectations clear, try something and return to see what followed. The Field Diary and other tools help you remember what you observed, what you decided and what remains open.

You can also use your own AI to explore both Spiralweb sites with a question of your own. If your service can open the sources and supports voice, you can work through even a long text aloud in your own language.

On spiralweb.earth you can learn about the places and people whose work gives rise to these questions. Papers holds the texts, provisional assumptions and tools that can help an inquiry move forward. When participants return to a place, the first question is what they themselves can do with what they learned. If they choose to share some of it, the library has a place for it. What people come to understand in the field can change what you find here.

In a Local Return, participants bring what they have learned back into the work at the place itself. A Field Return is an account they choose to prepare for sharing, with its origins, uncertainties and terms of use intact. This shelf is for accounts that participants choose to make public. People in other places, specialists, funders and public authorities can respond to the questions they raise. On the next visit, participants can see what that response made possible.
