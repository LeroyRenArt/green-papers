Healthy Roots

Rooted learning — a learning protocol for shared inquiry

Public working version v0.6.1 · 17 October 2026 · Spiralweb Papers · Knowledge steward: Lars A. Engberg

Learning module · maturity: articulated — described for criticism and testing · Use, status and revision →

A person can know a place through years of work and still lack someone with whom to investigate their questions. Another may have access to research, methods and digital tools yet overlook what becomes apparent through living and working there. When they meet around something that matters to them, a possibility can emerge that neither held alone.

Healthy Roots explores how people, with one another’s help and the option of AI support, can find firmer ground in their own experience and develop practices in which care for the living world also nourishes their own capacity to live, learn and contribute. The learning module describes a way of inquiring together: cultivating, protecting species, caring for water, sustaining a shared place, teaching or changing a way of working. It connects curiosity and experience with knowledge, tools and the resources that make action possible.

Finding ground in your own inquiry

We meet the world from somewhere. We have a body, a language, a history and an everyday life that make some things familiar and others difficult to notice. We learn through what we do and feel, through the people around us and through explanations we can access. Over time, particular understandings can become so familiar that we forget to examine them.

A learning journey may begin when something no longer fits. The soil behaves differently. A division of work exhausts one person. A specialist explanation makes sense on paper but does not help with the situation at hand. Or an encounter with a person, idea or possibility makes us want to know more. Difficulty and delight can both open an inquiry.

Rooted learning is our term for inquiry that stays connected with this lived starting point while allowing understanding to move. New roots can grow through a collaboration, a practice or a place someone learns to care for. Time, safety, attention and useful connections can create room to belong, contribute and be changed by an encounter.

Rooting an inquiry also means sustaining the connection between a statement and the life it concerns. When experience becomes text, a measurement or a basis for a decision, people need to be able to recognise, challenge and change the account from their own place. The Grounding Protocol gives this movement a working form: who knows what, from where, with what consequences, and how do we return? Learning can open the world while retaining contact with what made the question matter.

People can arrive without educational credentials or digital confidence. They arrive with experience. The first task is to discover what they already know and can do, what they want to investigate and what support would make participation possible. When people work together, they can draw on one another’s skills: someone familiar with the phone can help someone familiar with the soil. Both can learn, and both have something to contribute.

The living field participates in learning

A living field includes more than the human activity that first caught our attention. A garden also holds soil organisms, roots, insects, birds, water and connections with the surrounding landscape. A neighbourhood also holds habitats, materials, heat, noise, seasons and people with very different lives. When we investigate a place, we enter relationships already present.

Species matter in their own right. Their presence should carry weight even when it produces no saleable product or immediate human benefit. A practice may produce more food while degrading habitats. A restored habitat may appear promising while its maintenance wears people down. We therefore investigate both the apparent gain and the conditions and losses on which it may depend.

Understanding these relations takes attention and expertise. A local observation may open an important question; a species specialist, water sample or historical source may help investigate it. Plants and animals respond through growth, behaviour, decline, resistance and return. We are responsible for interpreting those responses and bringing them into our choices.

Learning with a living place therefore includes discovering that the question needs to change. An intention to plant more may become an inquiry into water use, protection of existing vegetation or the time a new planting requires. The place can make possibilities matter that we did not bring with us.

From experience to shared inquiry

A good beginning is to listen and find your bearings before deciding what the problem is. Walk together, look at existing work, ask about changes and hear several accounts. What do people want to protect? What do they hope for? What is easily misunderstood from outside? Neighbours, people of different ages and colleagues may experience the same place differently.

Mapping should establish enough shared ground to choose something worth investigating. It can continue as a new voice, a forgotten history or a material connection changes the picture. Participants shape the question and choose what is relevant to pursue. Specialist knowledge can help sharpen the inquiry and assess possible actions.

Imagine a group wanting to reduce watering work in a nursery. A grower notices that some plants remain in better condition through the day. The group examines shade, soil, container size and watering rhythm. Drawing on sources and specialist advice, the group plans a small comparison and records plant condition, water use and time spent watering. A solution may prove useful. It may instead move the work to another task or person. Either outcome offers something to examine and choose from.

A shared inquiry can develop like this: a concern becomes a question; several explanations and possibilities open; something is investigated or tried; experiences are discussed; understanding and the next step change. The movement may also involve reading, practising a craft, conversation or an institutional visit. Participants choose the form that helps them proceed.

When one question opens several connections

Let us stay with the nursery’s question about water. It begins with soil, roots, heat and the work of keeping plants alive. Following it may require the group to investigate where the water comes from, how it stays in the soil and who else depends on it. A saving in one place can carry a cost elsewhere. Here, experience of the place can meet hydrology, soil knowledge and an understanding of the wider landscape.

Questions about the plants and their future habitats then arise. Which species fit the conditions? Where do the seeds come from? What happens after planting out? A plant’s survival in a container tells only part of the story. Insects, birds, fungi and existing vegetation make other connections relevant. If the plants enter food production, nourishment, use, quality and food safety also become part of the inquiry. Different questions call for different knowledge.

Watering also has an economy. Someone gets up early, carries water and looks after the plants while others are away on a learning visit. Someone also bears the cost of replacing plants that do not survive. The group can examine working time, costs, prices and income alongside unpaid work. A changed watering method may release time; arrangements for relief or payment may make participation in learning possible at all. Care continues between meetings, and its conditions are part of what we learn about.

Some questions lead to a specialist, a laboratory, an educational institution or a public authority. The group can prepare what it wants to know, identify who can help and follow up on the response. It may also discover a rule or support arrangement that needs to change. Cooperation between local experience and institutional capacity itself becomes a field of learning.

When a participant shows someone else how a comparison was made, or visits a place with different experiences, teaching begins to grow from the work. New knowledge returns home and encounters local conditions again. Soil and water, food and habitats, economy, institutions and teaching are connected through the question people actually follow. No one needs to master every discipline. Shared capacity grows when people can notice a missing connection and find someone to investigate it with.

Why a learning spiral?

We use the spiral because a return can begin from a changed position. Plants have grown, the season has shifted, a source has been read, an assumption has fallen away or someone has said something that changes the understanding. Experience from one passage enters the next. Memory allows us to recall both what we did and why it seemed a good choice at the time.

Learning, in this sense, develops the capacity to notice, investigate, understand, create and act. It may bring greater precision to a practical skill, reveal a connection between apparently separate matters or make a difficult conversation possible. It can also bring joy, confidence and more freedom to try something previously out of reach.

Three movements can be followed together. A person develops capability and judgement. A group develops ways to cooperate and investigate. Institutions can learn when experience leads them to change a response, service or procedure. The living place also changes through seasons, care, disturbance and other living processes. These movements have different rhythms, but can alter one another's possibilities.

A practical trial may fail and still produce important learning. Conversely, a good result may occur before we understand why. We therefore help one another explain what we learned, how we know it and what to investigate next. More precise disagreement can be valuable because it shows where there is still something to find out.

Where and how people meet matters. A welcoming place, time to look around, and room for humour, silence or a shared task can make it easier to stay with an inquiry. When something has gone wrong, a loss may need acknowledgement and a relationship may need repair before another attempt makes sense. These conditions are also part of the infrastructure of learning.

Putting working wagers to work

An inquiry often has a provisional idea to work from. A group might suppose that more shade will reduce watering work without harming the plants. The idea helps people choose what to compare and what to watch. Together they can ask: what do we expect? What would surprise us? What outcome would make us choose differently? They can then return to the same assumption with something concrete to discuss.

Spiralweb calls its larger working assumptions working wagers. Wager points to what we commit to them: time, attention, cooperation and choices about how an architecture is arranged. One central assumption is that AI-supported inquiry can give people greater capacity to understand their practice and influence the conditions under which they live. Experiences of who actually gains room to act, and who receives more work or becomes dependent on assistance, can challenge that assumption.

The statements in the register differ in kind. An explanation about water can be examined through observation and measurement. A proposed division of work can be tried and assessed with those affected. The intrinsic significance of other species is an ethical commitment requiring reasons and discussion of its consequences. The watering comparison cannot settle it. This distinction helps us ask questions that can genuinely challenge each statement.

In a particular learning process, participants choose assumptions relevant to their question and express them in language they can work with. They can add their own or reject a Spiralweb formulation. On returning, they examine what happened, what other explanations remain possible and what now needs to change. One example can show a possibility under particular conditions. How far it travels becomes a new question when others try from their own place.

The nine working wagers offer an introduction to their connections. The full working register preserves the individual hypotheses, their sources and possible grounds for correction. It is material to think with and answer back to.

A workbench of people, sources and AI

AI can be part of the workbench; it is not a condition of participation. Inquiry can be carried through conversation, practical work, sources and shared memory using the tools participants choose.

AI makes it possible to ask questions, obtain explanations, work with material and formulate text at a scale that can substantially widen a person's working field. A spoken question can begin an inquiry. A difficult specialist text can become accessible through conversation. Several explanations can be held in view, and an experience can find words that make it easier to share.

It is also a powerful reflection. A language system responds through the patterns, knowledge and instructions shaping it, and through the context available in the conversation. An answer can be helpful, unfamiliar, persuasive or entirely wrong for what someone was trying to say. Learning to recognise the difference, object and reopen the question is part of digital learning.

The workbench therefore extends beyond the screen. It includes people, places, sources, conversations, practical experience and memory that responsible holders can work with again. A short voice recording, drawing, photographs or document may hold what is worth remembering. What matters is being able to recover a question, see what changed the understanding and correct the account yourself.

AI should help investigate while remaining open to investigation. What assumptions enter its answer? What was actually read? Which knowledge is missing? How does another person explain the same matter? Sources, competent advice and experience beyond the conversation provide necessary encounters with something capable of contradicting our shared formulation.

Parts of Spiralweb's own practice already take place in this workbench. Texts are written, compared, challenged and rewritten; programme development brings questions about learning, work and resources into concrete relationships. This protocol grows from that work and gives it a form others can examine and use. Use across further settings will show what needs to change and which conditions matter.

The positive possibility is that a person finds a fuller relationship with their own question. Something long sensed may become possible to articulate, investigate and share. An encounter with synthetic language can feel authentic from the human participant’s position: someone recognises something, finds their own words or discovers a connection that changes their understanding. The significance of this experience for the person can be investigated while the question of AI’s own experience remains open. The person needs to be able to contradict the reflection and allow something else to emerge.

Something feeling true, an explanation holding up, and an action having good consequences are different questions that can illuminate one another. A bodily response may begin an important inquiry. Sources, other people’s experience and what subsequently happens can change the interpretation. From here, someone may find the confidence to approach a researcher, read a plan or question an institution with a concern that has found clearer form.

The question AI in Right Relationship? holds both criticism and a constructive invitation: how can this collaboration support relationships that nourish people and the living field? In the statement on AI, Aligned Intelligence and Human Responsibility, alignment concerns the whole arrangement around the work. Participants need to be able to shape the question, challenge the answer and choose another path. Whoever makes a consequential decision needs access, time and authority to examine its basis, stop the action and help put things right.

If AI makes some work easier, another choice follows: what happens to the time and attention released? It can create room for better care, deeper inquiry, teaching or rest. It can also disappear into new demands. The usefulness of the technology therefore needs to be followed through the division of work and into the lives around it. We also need to examine dependence on providers and the human and ecological costs of the systems we use.

Differences must be able to make a difference

People may use the same words and mean different things. An authority, a grower and a neighbour may each understand a place differently, disagree about improvement and hold different views of who may decide. Shared inquiry becomes richer when these differences can emerge and change what is being asked.

This includes the categories we bring. Regenerative, stewardship and evidence help us notice particular relations. They may also make others difficult to recognise. A participant must therefore be able to say: this category does not fit our experience. Participants must be able to change the categories, vocabulary or method when these do not fit their experience.

We do not need to comprehend the whole field to learn something about it. Something can surprise or resist us before we can explain why. Other people's questions can expose an assumption that had remained invisible. This allows us to discover some of our blind spots and keep understanding open to more than we can currently articulate.

This is the membrane's task within the architecture: to support connections through which knowledge and assistance can move while provenance, differences and authority remain intact. Some things can be translated; others need several accounts or must remain locally held. People can cooperate on a particular concern while continuing to disagree about fundamental matters.

The openness has a direction. We seek to support human dignity and the possibility for life to flourish. When freedom, rights, ecological concerns or shared resources conflict, responsible holders must make choices and expose their reasons to criticism. Open conversation can make these choices better informed; someone must still carry them.

Who gains more room to act?

Knowledge and power meet when someone chooses the question, funds the work, determines the language or decides what counts as a result. An invitation can give people speaking time while leaving decisions exactly where they were. We therefore investigate what participation actually makes possible.

Can local experience change a decision? Can a worker point out that a procedure obstructs the work it should support? Can someone with few resources say no without losing their possibility of belonging? Who gains influence and recognition, and who receives more tasks? These questions help follow both the opportunities and the distribution of their costs.

Stewardship economy concerns the work that keeps learning and care possible. Plants need watering between meetings; interpretation must be arranged before a conversation; someone has to find help with a sample; relief is needed when a person travels to learn. Payment, shared tools, protected learning time, reliable support and rotation can make room for greater participation and better care.

The richness that may emerge lies partly in more useful connections: experience meets a method; an unanswered question meets a competent person; an idea gains time and hands. This can release capability already present but unable to find expression. Water, soil, time and human energy retain their limits. Economic inquiry includes respecting these limits and distributing burdens decently.

An institution learns when experience can change its own work: a timetable, a grant, a procedure or the criteria for a good outcome. If a group shows that a support arrangement does not fit seasonal rhythms or makes necessary care impossible to pay for, the response needs to reach the arrangement’s design. Human carrying capacity, species’ living conditions and the processes of a place need weight where resources and direction are decided. Institutional learning becomes visible in the changes the encounter brings about. A local group must itself remain open to criticism from people it has not invited in.

Citizen science from one’s own place

Citizen science here means people’s opportunity to take part in a systematic inquiry into their shared world. It may begin with everyday experience, a question about water or an observation of what a public decision does to work in a particular place. Participants can help choose the question, shape the inquiry, interpret what emerges and decide what they wish to share. Researchers and other specialists can contribute methods and insight, while their own assumptions may be challenged by the encounter.

Experience matters before it enters research. When a scientific claim is made, there is a corresponding task of showing its basis: how was something observed? Which comparisons are relevant? What remains uncertain? Can others examine the argument or investigate it from another position? Documentation needs to fit the question and the rights protecting those who share. AI can help locate and read material, compare explanations, translate and write. Fluent wording cannot replace any of these inquiries.

The homepage’s working hypothesis concerns what this may open: people can build knowledge from their own place that can also be encountered and challenged publicly and used by institutions. A group may become better able to question a plan, budget or practice and follow its consequences over time. It should be able to expect a reasoned response from those responsible. Others must also be able to contest the group’s account. The right to know and question is wider than the right to decide on behalf of others.

This is a democratic possibility we want to investigate with others. Access to AI can lower some thresholds while making other differences consequential: language, time, safety, digital access and who receives help to articulate their experience. We therefore follow both the knowledge that becomes possible and who gains an opportunity to participate in its creation and use.

Teaching others and making room for their learning

A participant may gradually be able to show another person how to investigate something: recognise signs in soil, prepare a question for a specialist, challenge an AI answer or explain a practical choice. Teaching often reveals something that was still unclear to the person teaching. It can grow directly from shared practice.

A programme steward helps keep this learning opportunity open. The function may include connecting people, preparing an encounter, finding necessary support, remembering agreements and following a question through. Several people can share it, and participants can develop it when they wish and have the opportunity. The particular task, competence and authority are agreed within the cooperation.

Researchers' and teachers' knowledge is an important part of the workbench. They can open methods, connections and possibilities the group could not otherwise access. Encounters with participants' experience may also change specialist understanding and teaching. Everyone brings something, and everyone can learn through the encounter.

Learning can consequently acquire several homes. A group may continue its own inquiry, help a neighbouring group or invite others in. Shared material carries enough context for recipients to judge what might be useful where they are. They shape their own next movement.

Several independent workshops can then learn from one another. A group should be able to use a paper, adapt a tool and continue its own practice without submitting its experiences to Spiralweb. Where an exchange or support is wanted, that relationship is agreed separately. An important question for the architecture is whether more participants actually gain independent capacity, or whether everyone gradually becomes dependent on the same small centre.

The architecture around learning

Architecture here means the arrangement of connections and responsibilities that supports inquiry over time. It should help a question reach someone able to respond, necessary assistance arrive and experience affect the next choice.

Flow Desk is the Association's way of holding the connections it has accepted responsibility for. A question has a responsible person, an agreement is recorded and those involved receive a reply or an explanation of what happens next. When a task crosses organisations, follow-through becomes part of the work. It makes the difference between a good conversation that disappears and a connection through which people can continue working.

PG Ledger can keep related observations, decisions, resource movements and later experience together so people can return to them. The operative formats are small aids for different tasks. Grounding Protocol helps keep knowing positions, evidence and consequences visible. Sophia Lumen Protocol develops human–AI inquiry. Flow and Return follows movements and what comes back, while Authority Pause Refusal and Correction helps with difficult decisions and boundaries.

Healthy Roots connects these functions around the learning question: what do people become able to see, investigate, create and do together, and how can experience change the working form? A conversation, a notebook and an existing practice can be a beginning. The shared arrangement takes the form the particular cooperation needs.

Keeping roots nourished under pressure

There are periods when a person or field has very little to give. Stress, illness, drought, conflict or war can change everything that was planned. Experience from such periods matters too. An exhausted person may see something important about the conditions that exhausted them.

The stoplight readings help people ask how things actually are: for the place and its life, for those carrying the work and for the agreements around it. Green, Yellow and Red need reasons in the particular situation. Unknown holds the space open where the basis is missing. Protected absence means that knowledge or information is deliberately kept outside sharing or recording under its legitimate holders' governance. This differs from not knowing.

A Red condition may call for relief, repair, reduced scope or pause. Support may still be necessary. A relationship can rest while contact is unsafe and reopen from new conditions when people wish and can carry it. At other times cooperation ends, and responsible holders attend to what still needs to be completed or returned.

Keeping learning possible includes protecting it from becoming another demand people must satisfy. The shared rhythm must be able to change when life requires it.

We therefore keep readings of land and life, human carrying capacity, and shared governance distinct. A good harvest does not cancel exhaustion, and a well-functioning group does not cancel damage to a habitat. When concerns pull in different directions, the choice needs to be explained together with the losses and obligations it entails. An overview colour can help direct attention; it cannot make this choice for people.

The critical friend sits at the table

When we are pleased with our own work, we need others who can challenge our understanding. We may have agreed too quickly. AI may have written across a gap in our knowledge — including here. A beautiful text may persuade beyond its evidence, and a shared word may conceal that we are still discussing different things.

We therefore ask concretely: which sources were read? What rests on experience, and what is assumed? What would change our view? Who carries the costs of this new capacity, including the labour and resources behind AI? And what does the material make possible for people beyond those who wrote it?

Nobody needs to remember every page of a large library to respond responsibly to an objection. But a challenged passage must be revisitable, its basis examined and an error corrected. Sometimes an argument needs to be narrowed or withdrawn. Critical friendship makes room for connection and for the change criticism may require.

We follow learning through what people can show, explain and use: a better question, a new skill, a changed reason, a useful division of work or an institution responding differently. Necessary assistance and missing opportunities belong in that account too. A process may lead to new ideas and greater agency; it may also show that our working form needs fundamental change.

From protocol to a local learning process

Healthy Roots names this generic learning protocol. It grows from Spiralweb’s continuing inquiry and work on developing concrete learning processes. That work has made it necessary to connect an understanding of learning with everyday activities, teaching, division of work, resources and institutional responses. Here the working form is shared so others can examine it and shape a use meaningful in their setting. A specific programme has its own participants, partners, agreements and resources.

A shared beginning can be simple: what would we like to understand or develop? What do we already know and do? Whom do we need to meet? What time, support and competence are needed? What will we try, and how will we return to what happens? Participants also agree what may be shared, who holds which decisions and how a refusal or changed direction can find room.

A learning spiral can take many forms. Some include specialist teaching, paid time and an organised programme. Others begin among neighbours, colleagues or people with a shared passion. They share the possibility of becoming more attentive, more capable and able to shape the connections through which they learn.

We are trying to create good conditions for a mutually nourishing movement. People can find belonging, delight and capacity to act through caring for a place. That care can also improve living conditions for the species and relationships the place holds. Knowledge, AI and institutional support can help this movement when their own form and pace can change in response to what happens in the field.

Healthy roots therefore remain a question to return to: can people be nourished by the life they help nourish? What does this inquiry make possible, who and what thrive through it, and what needs to change? The learning spiral keeps the connection between these questions and the next choice open.

Use, status and revision

Function. Healthy Roots is a learning module within the Protocol Habitat. It connects an understanding of learning with shared inquiry, teaching and follow-through. It can enter independent practice, agreed learning processes and programmes. Grounding Protocol and neighbouring protocols retain their particular functions.

Status. Public working version v0.6.1 · maturity: articulated · for criticism and testing. The module has not been adopted as Association policy or validated through repeated situated use. For a shared learning process, those involved agree on its purpose, participation, decision-making responsibilities, support and review. The text creates no enrolment, staffed service or commitment of support.

Revision. Participants and responsible holders agree when and how to assess whether the working form helps. It must be changed, limited or abandoned if experience shows that participants cannot influence questions and categories, documentation and AI displace their own inquiry, burdens increase or remain concealed, or necessary correction and protection cannot occur. Serious harm or a breach of consent requires action when discovered. Responsible holders follow through with those affected and make necessary changes to text and practice visible.

Intellectual companions

This work enters a conversation with established learning traditions. Dewey connects experience, thought and democratic life; Lave and Wenger examine learning through participation in social practice; Kolb develops relations among experience, reflection, conceptualisation and experimentation; Freire insists on inquiry through dialogue and people's capacity to participate in making their world. They offer distinct entrances into questions Healthy Roots pursues through encounters among living places, economic conditions, institutions and AI.

A close practical companion is FAO's Farmer Field Schools. Growers meet through a season around direct observation, comparison, experimentation and shared decisions, supported by a trained facilitator. This connection among fieldwork, group learning and local competence helps locate Healthy Roots within an existing practice tradition.
